Knowledge Graph, in plain language

A knowledge graph is a map of business ideas and how they connect. It is not a picture of database tables. Each circle is a meaning. Each line is a relationship with a name.

One example

A Dealer makes a Sale of a Vehicle to a Customer in a Region during a Year. That sale produces Revenue and Units Sold.

Read it as a sentence, not as a schema:

Dealer (who) → makes → Sale (what happened) → produces → Revenue (the result), and the sale happens in a Year (how we slice it).

The five groups on the graph

Every concept on the Semantic Atlas graph sits in one of these groups. The colors match the product.

Actor Who or what takes part.
Dealer, Customer, Vehicle, Salesperson.
Insurance: Customer, Agent, Policy.
Event Something that happened.
A Sale. A Claim.
Events are the facts the numbers are counted from.
Context The setting around an event.
Dealer network, Geography catalogue.
Context is the world the event sits in, not the result.
Outcome The number the business cares about.
Revenue, Units Sold, Loss Ratio.
“Sales” in a question usually means Revenue.
Attribute A way to describe or split a result.
Year, Region, Car Type.
“Per year” and “by region” are attributes.

Knowledge Graph spreads related ideas. Relationship Network makes busy ideas larger. Concept Graph fans each group into its own arc, like a glossary.

Three ways to look at the same map

View in the productWhat you seeWhy it helps
Knowledge Graph A living network. One circle per business concept. Lines have names such as “makes” or “produces”. The default view for a demo. You see the story, not a star schema.
Relationship Network The same links, but important concepts grow. Size follows how connected a concept is (degree, betweenness, or PageRank). Shows hubs. Sale and Revenue sit in the middle because many things depend on them.
Concept Graph A fan of meaning: Actors together, Events together, Outcomes together. Physical tables stay in the background. Good when the question is “what words do we use?”, not “how is the join written?”.

How a question uses the graph

Ask: show total sales per year

  1. Sales is a glossary word for the outcome Revenue, which is the sum of total_sales.
  2. Revenue is sourced from the event Sale.
  3. Per year is the attribute Year, taken from the sale date sales_date.
  4. The system writes SQL that sums sales and groups by the year of that date.
  5. You get a table and a chart. The graph is the map that made the path obvious.

If the question is vague (“show me sales”), the chat asks which outcome you mean instead of guessing.

How the graph is built

  1. A semantic pack describes one industry: Automotive or Insurance. It lists actors, events, measures, dimensions, glossary synonyms, and allowed relationships.
  2. The API turns that pack into a graph snapshot. Business entities become Actors. Fact tables become Events. Dimension tables become Context. Measures become Outcomes. Business dimensions become Attributes.
  3. The page hides the industry hub, so “Automotive” or “Insurance” is not a circle in the middle. It also keeps one circle when the same idea appears twice.
  4. d3-force pulls linked circles together and pushes others apart. React Flow draws the circles, the named lines, zoom, and fit.
  5. Pills above the graph (Actor, Event, Outcome, Attribute, Connected) highlight one group. They do not build a new graph. They dim the rest.

Why this helps

Shared language

“Sales”, “turnover”, and “revenue” point at one outcome. People and the AI chat use the same word.

Safe joins

A question can only walk links that exist. Revenue comes from Sale. Sale connects to Dealer and Region. The model does not invent a table.

A demo people can see

Leaders see Dealer, Sale, and Revenue as a story. They do not have to read a warehouse diagram to trust the answer.
