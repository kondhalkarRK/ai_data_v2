What is a Knowledge Graph?

It is a map of the things our business cares about and how they are connected. Think of it as the org chart of our data: instead of people and reporting lines, it shows business concepts — Customer, Vehicle, Dealer, Region — and the relationships between them.

Each circle is a business concept. Each arrow reads as a sentence.

Read every arrow as a sentence

CustomerownsVehicle
Vehiclesold byDealer
Dealerlocated inRegion

Put those three sentences together and you can already answer "Which regions sell the most vehicles to repeat customers?" — the map shows exactly how to get from Customer to Region.

Why we use it

Our data lives in dozens of systems and hundreds of tables with names like dim_carline or fact_claims. Nobody in the business should need to learn those. The Knowledge Graph translates them into the words we already use in meetings.

🗣️ One shared vocabulary

"Car", "Automobile" and "Car Line" all mean Vehicle. The graph shows one Vehicle and remembers the other names, so everyone gets the same answer.

🧭 Questions in plain English

When you ask Ask DB "revenue by region last quarter", it uses this map to know that Revenue comes from Sales, Sales are made by Dealers, and Dealers sit in Regions.

✅ Trustworthy numbers

Every KPI on the map is tied to the exact place it is calculated from. No more three versions of "Revenue" in three different decks.

🔍 See the whole business at a glance

New joiners, analysts and executives can explore how the business connects in minutes rather than weeks.

⚡ Faster decisions

Self-service answers instead of waiting days for a report request to come back.

🛡️ Change with confidence

Before changing a process or a system, see what else depends on it — which KPIs, which reports, which teams.

What problems it solves

A quick before-and-after for the questions leadership asks every week.

Without a Knowledge Graph

  • "Sales" means units to one team and revenue to another.
  • Analysts spend days finding which tables hold dealer, region and policy data.
  • Claims and policy data sit in separate silos; linking them is a manual project.
  • Reports disagree, and meetings are spent reconciling numbers.
  • Only a handful of experts know how the data fits together.

With a Knowledge Graph

  • Every KPI has one definition and one visible source.
  • Anyone can see that a Dealer sits in a Region and sells Vehicles.
  • Claim → Policy → Vehicle is a visible, ready-made path.
  • Ask DB answers business questions directly, with the same logic every time.
  • Knowledge is captured in the map, not in a few people's heads.

Knowledge Graph

Recommended

Purpose: explore the business as connected ideas, not tables. Every concept plays one role, and the roles read left to right: who takes part → the records we keep about them → what happens → what we measure.

Actor

Who or what takes part: Dealer, Vehicle, Salesperson, Region, Policy holder.

Entity

The master record behind an actor: Car Line, Dealer Network, Geography.

Event

Something that happens and is recorded: a Sale, a Claim, a Premium payment.

Attribute

A property to slice by: Date, Colour, Accounting Month.

Outcome

A result the business measures: Revenue, Units Sold, Loss Ratio.

🔗 Connected

Add this to any role to also show everything one step away, e.g. Outcome + Connected shows every KPI and the events it is measured from.

Knowledge Graph: a Vehicle (actor) is recorded as a Car Line (entity); a Sale (event) involves it; Revenue (outcome) is measured from the Sale.

Example: "How is Revenue produced?"

Click Revenue. It lights up Revenue → measured from → Sales Transaction directly, and one step further the Car Line, Dealer Network and Date around that sale. In one click you know Revenue can be broken down by car line, dealer and month.

Best for: exploring the domain, explaining the business model, discovering relationships you did not know existed.

Relationship Network

Purpose: find the hubs. Concepts are arranged on rings like a target: the most central one sits in the bullseye, and each ring outwards is less central. The top five are numbered so you can say "our #1 hub is the Sale".

The Sale sits in the bullseye: almost every KPI and entity connects through it.

Three ways to rank

Most connectedConcepts with the most direct links. Answers "what touches the most other things?"
BridgesConcepts that sit between groups. If a bridge breaks, parts of the business stop talking to each other.
Most influentialConcepts connected to other important concepts — the true centre of gravity.

Example

Switch to Bridges and Dealer Network moves towards the centre: it links Sales to Geography. That tells a programme manager that any change to dealer data will ripple into every regional report.

Best for: impact analysis, data-quality priorities, deciding where to invest first.

Simple Relationship View

Purpose: understand the model in thirty seconds. Every business concept appears once — "Car", "Automobile" and "Car Line" are one Vehicle tile — and every arrow carries its label, so the diagram reads as sentences.

Business Entities

Vehicle, Customer, Dealer, Region, Policy, Salesperson.

Business Events

A Sale, a Claim, a Premium payment.

KPIs

Revenue, Units Sold, Average Selling Price, Loss Ratio.

Time & Breakdowns

Date, month, quarter, year.

Grouped layout

Each category sits in its own shaded area. Blue arrows are business relationships (Sale → sold by → Dealer), pink arrows show where a KPI is calculated from (Revenue → measured from → Sale), amber arrows show breakdowns (Date → describes → Sale).

Top-down layout

The broadest concepts at the top, the figures calculated from them underneath — read it like a management report. Follow Revenue upwards: Revenue → Sales Transaction → Dealer → Region. That is exactly the drill path of a regional revenue report.

Top-down layout: Region at the top, KPIs at the bottom. Every arrow points up to what it belongs to.

Best for: meetings, onboarding, designing reports and dashboards, agreeing KPI ownership.

Real-world automobile examples

Five relationships that power most of our sales and insurance questions. Together they connect a customer to a claim in four steps.

CustomerOwnsVehicleWho drives what — the basis for loyalty and service campaigns.
VehicleSold ByDealerWhich dealer made the sale — drives dealer performance and incentives.
DealerLocated InRegionRolls dealer results up to regional targets.

From map to answer

What happens when you type a question into Ask DB.

1 · You ask

"Show revenue by region for SUVs last year."

2 · It understands your words

"Revenue" is a KPI. "Region" is a place. "SUV" is a kind of Vehicle. "Last year" is a time period.

3 · It follows the map

Revenue → measured from → Sale → sold by → Dealer → located in → Region, filtered to SUVs.

4 · You get the answer

A chart and table, plus a plain-English explanation of how the number was calculated.

Tips for exploring the graph

  • Click a circle to focus on it; click empty space to reset.
  • Pick a view at the top left: Knowledge Graph to explore, Relationship Network to find hubs, Simple Relationship View to read quickly.
  • Use the coloured filters to show only some roles or categories; in the Knowledge Graph add Connected to include their direct neighbours.
  • Drag circles to tidy a view for a presentation — the arrows follow.
  • "Business names" / "Technical names" switches between everyday names and the underlying system names for data teams.
  • Fit brings the whole map back into view.
